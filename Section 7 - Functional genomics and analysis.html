From reads to a defensible result

Section 6 ended with an experiment that could not separate treatment from batch, so no analysis could have rescued it. Here is an RNA-seq experiment designed so that cannot happen: both conditions represented in both batches, twelve libraries, and a treatment effect that is actually estimable.

Quality control told you the libraries are technically usable. It did not tell you what changed. That is the job here, and the last step is the hardest: turning a result into a sentence you can defend.

The counts are simulated. The analysis is not. Counts for 2,000 genes across 12 samples were drawn from a negative binomial, then normalised and modelled with a per-gene negative binomial GLM and a Wald test, adjusted with Benjamini-Hochberg, run once offline with the output embedded in this page. It behaves like a real result, including the false positives and the changes it misses.

After immune stimulation, which RNA abundances changed? Bulk RNA-seq measures the RNA molecules in a mixture of cells: an abundance difference can come from transcription, degradation, processing, or a change in the cell-type mixture, and steady-state RNA-seq cannot tell those apart on its own. What you get is evidence of changed abundance, not yet a mechanism.

What a variant is doing needs a different experiment: genotype-stratified expression, allele-specific expression, an eQTL design, or a perturbation. Nothing in these twelve libraries carries genotype information.

What the experiment actually is

The library preparation determines which RNA species can appear in the count matrix at all, and whether read direction identifies the transcript's strand. The details are below; the two sentences above are what they add up to.

What the library preparation decided

Almost none of the RNA in a cell is the RNA you want. Ribosomal RNA is "the most highly abundant component of total RNA isolated from animal or human cells and tissues, comprising the majority (>80% to 90%) of the molecules". Sequence total RNA and most of your run is ribosome. So most bulk workflows reduce that fraction, usually one of two ways. Not all of them do: some designs sequence total RNA deliberately, and the choice changes which RNA species can enter the library at all.

PolyA selectionOligo-dT beads capture anything with a polyA tail. Simple and cheap, and it shapes your experiment: the library is strongly enriched for mature, polyadenylated message, so non-polyadenylated species are heavily depleted. That means replication-dependent histone messages and many long non-coding RNAs are largely absent, and unspliced or intronic RNA is depleted rather than categorically excluded, which is why intronic reads still turn up in polyA data. It also needs intact RNA, so a degraded or archival sample will fail.
Ribosomal depletionProbes hybridise to the rRNA itself and pull it out. More expensive, keeps the non-polyadenylated transcripts, and tolerates degraded input, which is why clinical and fixed-tissue work uses it.

After that the RNA is fragmented and reverse transcribed to cDNA, and from that point on the whole thing is DNA chemistry: the same adapters, the same indexes, the same sequencer you met in Section 3. What arrives in your FASTQ is a DNA copy of a piece of whatever RNA survived the selection step, which in a polyA library is mostly mature message and in an rRNA-depleted library is a good deal more than that.

One more decision leaves a mark on the data. A read on its own does not say which strand of the genome the transcript came from, and for overlapping or antisense genes that ambiguity is expensive. A stranded library resolves it chemically, and there is more than one way to do it. The most common marks the second cDNA strand with dUTP so that it is not amplified in the final PCR, leaving the surviving molecules pointing the same way. Other kits preserve direction differently, so the relationship between read orientation and transcript strand is a property of the protocol rather than a universal rule. If you do not know which one your library used, you do not know how to count it, and every quantification tool will ask.

From reads to a number per gene

Once the reads are placed, the analysis stops being about sequence at all. Count how many reads landed in each gene, in each sample, and you have a table of numbers: genes down the side, samples across the top. Everything that follows is statistics on that table.

Twelve samples here: six stimulated and six not, and crucially three of each in each of the two batches. The question is which of the 0 genes that passed the count filter changed.

Ten genes from the table, chosen to show four kinds of row. Raw counts, exactly as they come out of the counting step.

These numbers are not yet comparable. The libraries were sequenced to different depths, so a bigger number can simply mean a bigger library, and the composition differs too: if a handful of genes take up much more of one sample's reads, every other gene in that sample is squeezed. Normalisation puts the samples on a common scale before anything is compared. Then the design formula tells the model which experimental effects it has to separate. Counts do not become a biological comparison until the sample labels and the model say how.

Go deeper: why the p-values are adjusted, and why fold change alone is a trap

You just ran one test per gene, so you ran thousands of tests. At a raw threshold of 0.05, thousands of tests produce hundreds of hits from nothing at all. Every axis on that plot uses an adjusted p-value for that reason, and Section 6 did the arithmetic.

The other trap is the opposite one. A gene with three reads in one group and nine in the other has a fold change of three, which sounds enormous and is measuring noise. That is why the vertical axis exists: the plot refuses to let you read a fold change without also reading how well it was measured.

And counts are not the kind of number a t-test was built for. They are discrete, their variance grows with their mean rather than staying constant, and with two or three replicates a variance estimated separately for each gene is too noisy to trust. So DESeq2 applies a Wald test on a negative binomial model of the raw counts, and edgeR a quasi-likelihood F test on the same model, in both cases after borrowing variance information across all the genes at once rather than trusting each gene's own three numbers.

Notice what that sentence does and does not say. DESeq2 and edgeR are software; the Wald test and the F test are the tests; the negative binomial is the model. Setting a package against a t-test compares three different kinds of thing. And the objection is to the naive t-test, not to t-statistics. limma-voom turns the mean-variance relationship into a weight on each observation and then runs an ordinary linear model, producing a moderated t-statistic whose variance has also been shared across genes, and it performs about as well. What all three have in common is not avoiding t-like statistics. It is refusing to estimate a variance from three numbers when twenty thousand genes are available to inform it.

And then: what are those genes doing?

A list of significant genes is not a result, it is a homework assignment. Pathway enrichment asks whether the list contains more genes from some known group than you would expect by chance, which converts a list of names into something you can say a sentence about.

Go deeper: what enrichment cannot tell you

It can only find what somebody already annotated. Gene sets are built from published work, so well-studied genes belong to many sets and poorly studied ones belong to none. A pathway can come out enriched because it is popular rather than because it is involved, and a gene nobody has studied cannot enrich anything.

The background matters and is usually wrong. Enrichment compares your list against all genes, but if your experiment could only detect expressed genes then the honest background is the expressed genes, not the genome. Using the wrong one inflates everything.

It is a hypothesis, not a mechanism. "Cytokine signalling is enriched" tells you where to look. It does not tell you what your variant does, and it never identifies a causal gene.

Postscript: the same count-matrix logic on peaks, and the other assays in this family

The same analysis, on peaks instead of genes

ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq end in peaks rather than counts per gene. Almost everything you have just done still applies, and the one place it does not is the interesting place.

A peak behaves like a gene. Agree a set of regions, count how many reads from each sample fall in each region, and you have a matrix with the same shape as a count table. The standard tools say so out loud: DiffBind "uses statistical routines developed in an RNA-Seq context (primarily the Bioconductor packages edgeR and DESeq2)". The volcano plot you just read, the adjusted p-values and the trap of ranking by fold change all carry over. The count-matrix idea transfers; the assumptions do not come with it. DiffBind's own documentation warns that normalisation methods developed for RNA-seq rest on assumptions that may not hold for ChIP or ATAC data, where the fraction of reads in peaks varies a great deal between samples, and that the choice of what to normalise against can change the answer more than the choice between edgeR and DESeq2. Defining the regions in the first place is a further set of assumptions that RNA-seq never has to make.

Then link peaks to genesA peak with no gene attached is hard to say anything about, so peaks get assigned to genes and the gene list goes through enrichment like the one you ran above. How that assignment is made varies, and it matters. ChIPseeker will annotate to the nearest gene; GREAT does something different, defining a regulatory domain around each gene with a basal region plus an extension and then testing enrichment over regions rather than over genes, which is the point of it.
Or ask what binds thereThis is the analysis a gene list cannot support. Take the sequence under each peak and ask which transcription factor motifs are over-represented against a matched background: HOMER and the MEME suite do that, against databases such as JASPAR. chromVAR is not that. It computes a bias-corrected measure of how much accessibility deviates, per cell or per sample, across all the peaks sharing a motif, so it is answering "is this factor's activity changing here?" rather than "is this motif unusually common in this list?"
The place it is easiest to go wrong. Your genes were defined before the experiment existed. Your peaks are defined from the data you are about to test, and if you define them the wrong way the arithmetic stops holding. The authors of csaw are specific about which way is wrong: "calling peaks in individual libraries or treatment groups can lead to loss of error rate control during the DB analysis. This is because the definition of the regions to be used for DB testing is not independent of the DB status of those regions."

Read that precisely. It is a warning about calling peaks per library or per treatment group and then testing between those groups, not a claim that every region set derived from the data is invalid. Defining regions once from the pooled data, without reference to the comparison, is a normal and defensible thing to do. The choice between the union of every sample's peaks and only the peaks they share still changes the answer, and neither is right in general.
Two caveats on the peak analyses, both worth knowing before you believe one

Nearest gene is a biased assignment, not a neutral one. It was the motivating example for GREAT: assigning regions to the nearest gene "introduces a strong bias toward genes that are flanked by large intergenic regions", so that a term covering 14% of human genes ended up associated with over 33% of the genome, producing "numerous false positive enrichments". And enhancers genuinely skip past their neighbours to reach a target further away, which no distance rule can capture. Contact-based and co-accessibility methods exist for exactly this reason.

A motif nominates a family, not a protein. A motif is a position weight matrix, a summary of the bases a factor tends to sit on, and close relatives share them: as the ATAC analysis guide puts it, "not all TFs have identified motifs and TFs from the same family can share very similar motifs". So "a GATA motif is enriched in these peaks" is a real result and it does not tell you which GATA. Deciding that needs expression data, a ChIP experiment, or a perturbation.

For ATAC there is a further move, footprinting: a bound protein blocks the transposase, so an occupied site shows a dip in insertions inside an otherwise open region. The catch is that Tn5 has its own sequence preference, so an uncorrected footprint can be the enzyme rather than the factor, and bias-correcting tools are not optional.

ChIP-seq in full, because it is the one that is hardest to picture

Formaldehyde is added to living cells, which freezes proteins onto the DNA they are touching. The cells are broken open and the chromatin sheared, usually by sonication, to fragments of roughly 100 to 300 bases. Then an antibody against one protein, or one histone modification, is used to pull that protein out of the mixture, bringing whatever DNA it was crosslinked to with it. The crosslinks are reversed, the protein is digested away, and what you sequence is the DNA. Never the protein.

So the antibody is the entire experiment. ENCODE's own guidelines list the two ways it fails: "poor reactivity against the intended target and/or cross-reactivity with other DNA-associated proteins". Neither failure produces an error message. Both produce peaks.

This is why ChIP needs an input control, and why the other two assays on this page do not. Shearing is not uniform: "DNA breakage during sonication is not uniform. In particular, some regions of open chromatin are preferentially represented in the sonicated sample". So open chromatin looks enriched whether or not your protein was there, and so do regions present at higher copy number. An input is the same chromatin, crosslinked and sheared identically, with the antibody step left out. ENCODE requires one: "Each ChIP-seq experiment should have a corresponding input control experiment with matching run type, read length, and replicate structure." Without it you are comparing your peaks against an assumption of uniformity that is known to be false.

The distinction worth carrying away, stated carefully. An RNA-seq experiment counts into genes that were defined before you started, so the object being counted is far more stable than a peak and two laboratories have a much better chance of comparing numbers.

"Far more stable" is not "identical", and treating gene counts as automatically comparable is its own mistake. Which annotation release you used changes gene models and boundaries; whether the library was stranded changes what counts for overlapping genes; what you did with reads matching several places changes the totals; and how transcripts were aggregated to genes changes them again. The methods section still has to say all of that.

A peak is not like that. You created it, by choosing a caller, a threshold and a control. Change any of those and the peaks move, split, merge or disappear, and your "same" region is a different object. That is why peak-based results are harder to compare across studies than expression results, and why the methods section of an ATAC or ChIP paper has to say far more than "we called peaks".
