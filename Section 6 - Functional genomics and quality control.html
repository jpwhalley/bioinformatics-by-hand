Quality control: what can this experiment still answer?

Earlier sections let you inspect individual reads, alignments and variants. Here you have twenty four ATAC-seq libraries, each represented by a handful of imperfect summaries. The task is not to find a magic cutoff. It is to decide whether each library, and the comparison it belongs to, is still interpretable.

Simulated libraries. The thresholds, the protocol comparisons and the arithmetic are real, and each is quoted with the source that set it.

Sections 4 and 5 were genetics: what is in this genome, and which of it matters. From here the question changes. Functional genomics asks what the cell is doing with the genome, which is a question about activity rather than sequence: which regions are open, which proteins are bound, which genes are being transcribed and how much. It is a family of assays rather than one assay, and they share a pipeline. This section takes the first of them, ATAC-seq, as far as deciding which samples you are allowed to use. Section 7 takes RNA-seq the rest of the way.

ATAC-seq measures where Tn5 inserts more often than background. That is a statement about a population of cells: accessibility is probabilistic and averaged over however many nuclei went into the reaction, so an ATAC peak is evidence that a region is more often open, not proof that a gene there is active.

The reads themselves match the genome plainly. This is DNA, so an ordinary aligner places it, with none of the trouble RNA will give you in Section 7. What separates a good ATAC library from a bad one is not whether the reads match, but where they land, and how their distribution compares with what you expected. Every metric in this section measures that without looking at individual reads, because at twenty four samples, let alone twenty four hundred, looking has stopped being available to you.
The match in this sectionReads still match the genome. The new comparisons are distributions: insertion sites against expected background, and each library against its replicates and the design it belongs to.

Quality control is not a step. It is a gate at every junction

A pipeline is a chain of things that turn one file into another, and each link can fail in a way the previous check could not have seen. So there is no single moment called quality control. There are three, at least, and this section is the middle one.

On the raw readsFASTQ
Per-base quality, adapter content, duplication, whether the pool demultiplexed. You did this in Section 3, by eye, and in Section 4 with FastQC. Catches a run that failed, a library whose inserts were shorter than the read, and a pool that will not sort into samples. Nothing here knows anything about your genome.
Mapped and filtered fragmentsBAM
Alignment rate, duplicate rate, insert size distribution, mitochondrial fraction, and TSS enrichment, which is the one signal-to-noise measure available at this stage because it needs only an annotation of start sites. Catches reads that were perfectly good and landed in the wrong places, or in no useful place. A library can be flawless on the first gate and dead on this one, which is exactly what happened to B12 above.
Peaks, counts and cohortpeaks, counts, matrix
How many peaks were called, FRiP, whether replicates agree, how the samples cluster, and whether the design is balanced. FRiP belongs here and not on the gate above: it is the fraction of usable reads falling inside called peaks, so it cannot exist until peaks do, and it moves when the peak caller's settings move. Catches a sample that mapped beautifully and still measured almost nothing, and a batch effect no per-sample metric can see, because it is a property of the comparison rather than of any one sample.
Each gate exists because the one before it is blind to something. Read quality cannot see where a read went. Alignment rate cannot see whether the reads that aligned landed anywhere informative. TSS enrichment cannot see how the samples relate to each other. And nothing computed on a single sample can see a batch effect, which is why the last gate looks at the samples together rather than one at a time.

That last point is the one this section builds to. Quality is a property of the comparison as well as of each sample. Every library in a cohort can pass every per-sample check and the experiment can still be unable to answer the question it was designed for.

What the experiment actually is

You cannot judge data from an assay you cannot picture. This is one paragraph of bench work, and every quality metric in this section follows from it.

ATAC-seq is the "assay for transposase-accessible chromatin". The reagent is Tn5 transposase, pre-loaded with sequencing adapters. Put it into permeabilised nuclei and it does two jobs in one reaction: it cuts the DNA, and it ligates adapters onto the ends it just cut. Buenrostro's protocol paper puts it in one sentence: "The transposase simultaneously fragments the DNA and inserts sequence handles that are then used for amplification during library preparation." That combined step is called tagmentation, and it is why an ATAC library can be made in a few hours from fifty thousand cells.

The measurement is a consequence of the enzyme being large. Tn5 is a protein complex, and DNA wrapped around a nucleosome or covered by a bound protein is harder for it to reach, so insertion there is less probable rather than impossible. It cuts more often where the chromatin is open, and where it cut is the experiment. Nothing is being detected; something is being reached, more often in some places than others. That probabilistic wording matters: ATAC-seq measures an aggregate over every nucleus in the reaction, so a peak means a region was open in more of those cells, not that it was open in all of them.

The library is ordinary DNA.An ordinary aligner places it. There is no splicing to allow for, which is what makes Section 7 harder rather than this.
The cut site is offset.Tn5 works as a dimer and its two active sites cut nine bases apart, so the field adds 4 bases to plus-strand read starts and subtracts 5 from minus-strand ones. A published pipeline does this for you and never mentions it.
Fragment lengths come in bands.Sub-nucleosomal fragments under about 100 bases, then peaks near 200, 400 and 600 as a fragment spans one, two or three nucleosomes. The distribution is a picture of chromatin, drawn by an enzyme that could not get through it.
Mitochondrial DNA is a soft target.It carries no histone nucleosomes, though it is not naked either: it is packaged into TFAM-containing nucleoids. What really drives the problem is copy number, because a cell holds hundreds to thousands of mitochondrial genomes against two copies of the nuclear one, and how much you get also depends on cell type, how hard the lysis was and how cleanly the nuclei were prepared. Hold on to that: it explains the second half of this section.

How that number is actually computed, and why two groups can disagree about it

ENCODE's definition: collect the reads around a reference set of transcription start sites into one aggregate distribution "extending to 2000 bp in either direction (for a total of 4000bp)", then normalise by "the average read depth in the 100 bps at each of the end flanks of the distribution (for a total of 200bp of averaged data)". That is why the flanks sit at 1. A TSS enrichment of 9 means nine times the signal in those flanks, two kilobases either side of the start site. It is a fold change over a chosen background, not over "nowhere in particular", and moving the background moves the number, which is exactly what the next paragraph is about.

The documentation and the code do not agree. The portal says the metric is "the signal value at the center of the distribution after this normalization". The pipeline's own encode_task_tss_enrich.py reports max(bam_array.mean(axis=0)), the highest point of the profile. Those two definitions coincide only when the profile's apex sits exactly on the start site, and it often does not: in the simulated profiles on this page the apex is slightly upstream, so the two definitions return different numbers. Do not read that offset as a universal fact about real libraries. It varies with the annotation used, the cell type and the Tn5 shift correction, and the point being made is about the discrepancy between a definition and its implementation, not about which direction the peak leans.

And a different paper computes it differently again. The Omni-ATAC paper normalises against the left flank only: "the signal from bases −2,000 to −1,800 was used to represent the background signal". Same libraries, same reads, different number. When you read "TSS enrichment 6.2" in a methods section, you have not been told enough to reproduce it.

Now move the line

Most quality control is done sample by sample. Every library gets its own set of numbers and its own verdict, and a library that fails is excluded outright: it never reaches Section 7, and nothing downstream will remind you it existed. So one question gets asked twenty four times: is this one good enough to keep?

The same three gates, on the other assays in this family

RNA-seq. The first two gates are the same. The third asks a question this section does not: how many genes were detected at all? A sample that mapped well and then detects far fewer genes than its neighbours has low-complexity or degraded RNA, or was sequenced too shallowly to see anything but the abundant transcripts. There is no published number for this, and there should not be: it depends on the tissue, the depth and the annotation, so it is read against the other samples in the same experiment rather than against a threshold. ENCODE does set one for agreement instead, asking for a Spearman correlation above 0.9 between isogenic replicates and above 0.8 between anisogenic ones.

ChIP-seq. Same first two gates, and then the assay's own signal-to-noise measures: FRiP, and the strand cross-correlation statistics NSC and RSC. It also carries a requirement the other assays do not, because its third gate is a comparison rather than a measurement: an input control, without which a peak cannot be distinguished from a region that shears easily. Section 7 explains why.

The pattern is worth more than the metrics. Every assay checks the reads, then checks where they landed, then checks whether what came out the far end is measuring anything. Only the middle metrics are assay-specific.

What a threshold actually is

Three things are true of the number you have just been moving, and none of them is visible from seeing that number on a report.

1. ENCODE grades and audits. It does not make your inclusion decision.

The ATAC-seq standards have no column called "fail". They have a column called Resulting Data Status, whose values are Ideal, Acceptable and Concerning. The wording is hedged throughout, on purpose: alignment rate "should be greater than 95%, though values >80% may be acceptable"; FRiP "should be >0.3, though values greater than 0.2 are acceptable", and then, in the same sentence, "for EN-TEx tissues, FRiP scores will not be enforced as QC metric".

Do not push that too far, though. ENCODE also runs audits, and those are less gentle than the bands: a dataset with TSS enrichment below 5, or FRiP below 0.2, gets flagged as not compliant. So the accurate statement is not that ENCODE never judges. It is that ENCODE grades and audits data against its own production standards, and does not make the scientific inclusion decision for your study. That decision is yours, it depends on what you are asking, and nobody else can be blamed for it.

The ChIP-seq guidelines say out loud where such numbers come from. They were "constructed based on the historical experiences of ENCODE ChIP-seq data production groups with the purpose of balancing data quality with practical attainability", and are "routinely revised".

And they move under you. An earlier version of this section quoted the ATAC page as asking for "15 million non-duplicate, non-mitochondrial aligned reads for single-end sequencing and 50 million for paired-ended sequencing", and pointed out that fifty million paired-end reads is twenty five million fragments, not fifteen: the page contradicted itself. Checked again on 18 August 2026, it now reads "25 million non-duplicate, non-mitochondrial aligned reads for single-end sequencing and 50 million for paired-ended sequencing (i.e. 25 million fragments, regardless of sequencing run type)". The inconsistency is gone and the number this section filters on went up by two thirds.

That is not a criticism of ENCODE, who say themselves that these are "routinely revised". It is the reason to record the version and the date of any standard you applied, because a methods section that says "libraries meeting ENCODE standards were retained" does not say which standard, and the answer changed while this page was being written.

A published threshold is a consortium's working compromise, written down. Treating it as a law of nature is a decision you made, not one they made for you.

2. The number everyone quotes has no threshold, and the one with a threshold has moved.

Ask anyone what you check in an ATAC-seq library and you will hear "the mitochondrial fraction". ENCODE sets no threshold for it. The word appears in the standards once, inside the phrase "non-duplicate, non-mitochondrial aligned reads", and the pipeline discards chrM before it counts anything: the released files are named ...nodup.no_chrM_MT.... The fraction is reported and nothing is decided on it.

Meanwhile TSS enrichment, which does have a threshold, is quoted against an annotation that has moved. The ENCODE4 table labels its rows "GRCh38 Refseq TSS annotation". The file the pipeline actually ships for hg38 is hg38_gencode_v29_tss_unique.zero_based.bed.gz, which is GENCODE, not RefSeq. Between two versions of the pipeline's genome data that file was also reissued in zero-based coordinates, a one base shift that moves every sample's score without a single read having changed.

So the metric with no threshold is the one everybody checks, and the metric with a threshold is checked against a file that is not the one the threshold names. "Everyone does this" and "the standard requires this" are different claims, and they are constantly confused.

3. A metric can expire, because it was measuring the protocol.

You just watched the mitochondrial fraction fall from around half a run to a few percent across three protocol generations, without anybody improving their bioinformatics. A metric that separated good libraries from bad ones in 2013 separates almost nothing in a laboratory running Omni-ATAC in 2026, because the thing it was detecting has largely been engineered away.

That is worth holding on to, because it is not special to this metric. Duplicate rate is another: the Corces laboratory's own protocol paper says "we do not use this metric as a way to determine library quality", and points out that it fails in both directions. A library from dead cells gives you high complexity and a low duplicate rate, and is still a bad library.

A quality metric is a hypothesis about how experiments fail. When the way they fail changes, the metric quietly stops being about quality and starts being about which protocol somebody used. Nothing in the report says so, and the threshold stays in the pipeline for years.

And then you test twenty thousand times

Suppose every library passed and the design survived. You are about to compare the two arms across 20,000 genes, which is what the volcano plot in Section 7 does. At the conventional 5% threshold, the number of genes you would expect to call significant if the treatment did nothing whatsoever is:

A thousand hits from an experiment where nothing happened. That is not a subtle statistical point, it is arithmetic, and it is why the volcano plot in Section 7 is drawn against an adjusted p-value rather than a raw one. Genome-wide association uses a threshold near 5 times ten to the minus eight, which is roughly 0.05 divided by the number of independent tests.

Notice what this shares with the threshold you just moved. In neither case is any single number wrong. In both, the number of chances you gave yourself changed what the numbers mean, and nothing in the output says so.
