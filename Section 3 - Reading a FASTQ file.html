Reading raw reads

A sequencer gives you base calls, quality scores and sample indexes. Inspect four FASTQ reads, decide what to keep or trim, then assign pooled reads back to their samples. Three questions run through it:

  1. What bases did the instrument call?
  2. How much confidence should you place in each call?
  3. Which sample does this read belong to?

Anatomy of one FASTQ record: 4 lines

@ identifier
begins with @ and identifies the read. What it contains is up to the software that wrote the file; it is not fixed by the format. Illumina-style identifiers commonly encode run and cluster coordinates.
sequence
the bases the instrument called, normally A, C, G, T or N.
+ separator
begins with + and separates sequence from quality. It may stand alone, or repeat the identifier.
quality
one encoded quality symbol per called base. Once any line wrapping is removed, the quality and sequence strings must be the same length.

Where that adapter came from

Read D above is a perfectly good read that stops being your genome partway through.

Nothing goes onto a sequencer as you made it. Adapter sequences are added to both ends during library preparation, so the molecule on the flow cell is adapter + your fragment + adapter, and the machine reads a fixed number of bases inward. Read D is 75 accurately-called bases, and not all of them are yours.

Searching for AGATCGGAAGAG
Read D is 75 bases. Some of them are the sample-derived insert and some of them are Illumina adapter. Find the boundary.

The insert was shorter than the read. The machine read through your insert and out into the adapter on the other side. Illumina put it plainly: "When read length exceeds DNA insert size, a run can sequence beyond the DNA insert and read bases from the sequencing adapter."

Notice what the quality scores did about this. Nothing. Every one of those adapter bases is Q38 or better, because the machine read them correctly. They are real bases, accurately called, and none of them is yours.

A high Phred score means the instrument is confident about the base it called. It does not mean the base came from the sample-derived insert.

What an adapter carries, and how it gets there

Adapters are added by ligation (TruSeq), transposition (Nextera-style tagmentation) or amplification with tailed primers, and they are what makes sequencing possible. They carry: P5 and P7, the sequences that let a fragment bind the flow cell and form a cluster; Sequencing primer sites, where the machine starts reading; and An index, the short barcode saying which sample the fragment came from, which is the one that matters for the rest of this section.

The probe used here is the short shared stub trimming and screening tools look for; adapter detection is a specified query sequence and a matching rule, chosen for the tool's purpose.

One flow cell, many samples

A sequencing run produces far more reads than any single sample needs. So you do not run one sample. You give every library a different barcode, mix them, and sort the reads out afterwards by reading the barcode.

The barcode sits inside the adapter, so it is attached to every fragment from that library before anything is pooled. The machine reads it separately from your insert, in a short index read. In this Illumina-style example the converter records what it saw in the header line of every FASTQ record:

@SIM-RUN1:1:1107:26433:14103 1:N:0:TCCGGAGA

Instrument, run, flow cell, lane, tile and coordinates, then read number, whether the read was filtered out, a control field, and last, the index the machine read. That last field is the whole of what the computer knows about which sample this read belongs to.

Demultiplexing is another matching problem. Compare an observed index with the sample sheet under a mismatch rule, then decide whether the best match is unique enough to use. That is Section 1, with an eight-base read and a four-entry reference: the same operation, on a smaller reference, run a billion times before your analysis starts.
Why the tolerance cannot simply be turned up

To correct n substitution errors and still know which sample you meant, every pair of indexes has to differ by at least 2n + 1 bases. With one mismatch allowed that is three. Whether the converter makes you obey it is a configuration question rather than a law: strict collision checking enforces the distance and refuses to start the run, while the relaxed checking that recent BCL Convert versions default to will accept index sets without that guarantee. The bound is the same either way; what changes is whether software stops you.

Many Illumina index sets are designed with a pairwise distance of at least four, and the vendor still advises that two mismatches can be used "to maximize data recovery". Four is less than 2(2) + 1, so at two mismatches the correction is no longer guaranteed unique; the advice rests on measured misassignment rates rather than on the bound. Recognise it for what it is: a threshold justified by how often it goes wrong, not by a proof that it cannot.

The failure demultiplexing cannot see: index hopping

Everything above assumed the index on a read is the index that was ligated to it. Sometimes it is not. Free adapter left in the pool, and the chemistry used on patterned flow cells, can cause a fragment to be sequenced carrying a different sample's index, perfectly. There is no mismatch. There is nothing to detect.

How often it happens varies with flow-cell chemistry and library preparation, and the fix is structural rather than analytical. Unique dual indexes give every sample its own i7 and its own i5, making many hopped combinations invalid: those reads land in the undetermined pool rather than silently in another sample. They mitigate index hopping; they do not prove that every surviving read has the origin its index claims.

How a symbol becomes a score (the Phred math)
A quality symbol is decoded as Q = ASCII_value − 33 (the "Phred+33" offset used by Sanger / Illumina 1.8+). The score is a log-scaled error probability: P(base is wrong) = 10^(−Q/10). So Q20 → 1 in 100 (99% accurate), Q30 → 1 in 1000 (99.9%), Q40 → 1 in 10,000. Example: the symbol I is ASCII 73 → Q40; # is ASCII 35, so it encodes Q2: about a 63% predicted error rate, not the even chance the symbol is often described as. A call like that is effectively uninformative. Select any quality symbol above to see the arithmetic computed live.
Why does quality fall off toward the 3′ end? (Read B)
Illumina reads a cluster base-by-base through cycles of chemistry. With each cycle the millions of molecules in a cluster drift slightly out of sync ("phasing") and signal-to-noise decays, so late cycles, the 3′ end of the read, are less certain. That is why quality trimming clips the 3′ end, and why paired-end R2 is usually a bit worse than R1. Adapter trimming also acts on the 3′ end, for a different reason: a short insert reads through into adapter, as read D showed.
Q20 vs Q30: what people actually mean by "%≥Q30"
Sequencing-run reports quote the fraction of bases at or above Q30. It is a compact run-level summary, and it is not a universal pass mark: what counts as normal depends on the instrument, the chemistry, the read length and the application. Compare a run against the specification for the platform it was run on rather than against a remembered number. It's a coarse health metric: a read that's Q40 for 60 bases then Q5 for 15 can still average out "fine," which is exactly why you look at the per-base profile (above) and not just the mean.
