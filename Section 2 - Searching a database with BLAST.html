The three numbers in the table

Raw score: agreement under this scoring scheme, interpretable within it but not directly comparable across schemes.
Bit score: the raw score with the scheme normalised out, comparable across compatible searches.
E-value: the expected number of chance alignments scoring at least this well, in a search this size. Near zero means the similarity is no accident of searching. It says nothing about why the sequences are similar.

Go deeper: how BLAST searches

Smith–Waterman, the rigorous algorithm, is O(mn): too slow for a multi-billion-base database. BLAST is a heuristic: find short exact seed matches first, then extend each seed in both directions, dropping any extension whose score falls too far below its running maximum (the X-drop rule). Seed length is set by task, not alphabet: blastn 11, megablast 28, blastn-short 7 for primers, blastp 3 for proteins. Longer seeds are faster and less sensitive. Whatever survives the pruning gets a score and an E-value. Bigger databases and longer queries give chance more opportunities, so the same score is less impressive on a bigger search.

Similarity is not orthology. A low E-value argues the similarity is not a random search result; a strong hit is consistent with homology, shared ancestry, but that remains a biological inference. The stronger claim of orthology, the same gene in two species, also needs gene-family and species-tree context, and no single BLAST table can settle it.
Go deeper: how this page simplifies real BLAST

Real BLAST chooses its seed length by task (11 for blastn, 28 for megablast, 7 for blastn-short), scores proteins with a matrix estimated from real alignments, and can open gaps. This page uses k=4 seeds, so they are easy to spot in a 41 bp database sequence, and it extends without gaps.

What is not simplified is the statistics. The scoring scheme, +2 for a match and −3 for a mismatch, is a reward and penalty pair real blastn offers, and the bit scores and E-values are computed with the published statistical constants for that scheme, so the numbers in the table are the numbers the real tool would report for the same alignments. The one thing that is toy-scale is the search space: these E-values are computed against 410 bases of database. Real nucleotide databases hold hundreds of billions of letters and keep growing, and because the E-value scales with search space, the same alignment scores a far larger E-value there. Make the database bigger and every E-value grows with it.

Go deeper: why % identity is not enough on its own

A 30 bp alignment at 80% identity (24/30 matches) sounds great until you remember that under a toy model with independent, equally frequent bases, chance identity is already 25%, a random baseline of 7.5 matches. The bit score, and therefore the E-value, account for this; raw % identity does not.

So read a hit in three parts, in this order. One, the E-value: could a score like this turn up by chance in a search this size? Two, the alignment length and how much of the query it covers: a spectacular match to 6 bases of a 300 base query has explained almost nothing. Three, the identity, together with what you know about the biology: how similar is the part that did align, and is that similarity meaningful for these two organisms and this gene? A hit with 100% identity over 6 bases passes the third test and fails the first two, which is why no one of the three is enough alone.
