Which variant matters

Section 4 left you with variants supported by the reads. Genome-wide, a typical human genome differs from the reference at roughly four to five million sites. Most of those differences are genuine, and almost none of them are relevant to the question being asked.

Your task is not to find the most dramatic annotation. It is to decide which variants remain plausible under a stated disease model.

The pipeline is finished, and it was never the hard part. You now have to get from five million true statements to the one or two worth acting on, and every step you take to do that is a hypothesis about what you are looking for. Choose the wrong hypothesis and you will filter away the answer without ever seeing it.

The strongest exclusion in this model

Every other filter rests on a prediction, an annotation or an assumption about the biology. This one rests on arithmetic, and it is worth doing by hand once so you know why it is the first thing anyone reaches for. It is not certainty: it inherits the disease model, and if the model is wrong so is the bound.

the condition affects 1 in 50,000 people
the model says affected people are heterozygous, so they occur at about 2q
so 2q <= 1/50,000, giving q <= 1 in 100,000, or 0.001%
FBN1 has an allele frequency of 2.1%
which is about 2,100 times that upper bound

Watch the units, because this is where the mistake usually happens. 2.1% is an allele frequency, not a proportion of people. The proportion carrying at least one copy is 1 − (1 − 0.021)², which is 4.16%, or about 1 person in 24. Reading 2.1% as "1 in 50 people" conflates the two, and the error runs in both directions: the recessive note below is where it usually runs the other way.

Notice the direction of the evidence. Being common can rule a variant out under this model. Being rare cannot rule one in: almost every variant in the genome is rare, and so is almost every variant that explains nothing.

That argument needs no tool, no prediction and no assumption about the gene. It is counting. It is also why a frameshift is not automatically interesting: severity tells you what a variant does to a protein, and frequency tells you whether it could possibly be causing a rare disease. Only one of those is evidence about your patient.

Notice what the five filters are asking. Some evidence asks whether a variant could disrupt biology at all: the predicted consequence, the conservation of the position, the in-silico prediction. Other evidence asks whether that disruption is compatible with this disease model and this family: the population frequency, and which parent carries it. The gene list sits across both, because it is a comparison between the gene and the child's presentation rather than a property of the variant.

You need both kinds, and no single annotation is a verdict. Real frameworks such as ACMG/AMP combine population data, predicted consequence, phenotype fit, de novo occurrence, segregation and functional work, weighting each as evidence rather than sorting them into two boxes. The reason FBN1 is about to catch you is not that its annotations are a lesser kind of evidence. It is that they are all true and none of them is about this child.

Go deeper: where this argument stops working

Recessive conditions. If two copies are needed, carriers are unaffected and the allele can be far more common than the disease. For a recessive condition at 1 in 50,000, q = √(1/50,000) = 0.447%, and the carrier frequency 2q(1−q) is 0.890%, which is about 1 person in 112.

Those two numbers are easy to swap, and swapping them is the same unit error as reading 2.1% as "1 in 50 people". 0.890% is the carrier frequency, not an allele frequency. A single causal allele could sit at up to about 0.447% under this deliberately simplified one-allele model, and usually a good deal lower, because most recessive conditions are caused by many different alleles sharing that budget between them.

Late onset and incomplete penetrance. If only a fraction of carriers ever develop the condition, the allele is correspondingly more common than the disease. In adult-onset disease this is the rule rather than the exception.

Population matters. A variant rare globally can be common in one population, and frequency databases are still unevenly sampled. A variant absent from a database may be genuinely rare, or may come from a population nobody has sequenced enough of.

All four of those move the same number. The bound you can defend depends on the inheritance model, the penetrance, the prevalence and how many different alleles cause the condition, and there is a published way of doing it properly rather than by the back of an envelope: Whiffin and colleagues set out the maximum credible population allele frequency in exactly these terms. The envelope calculation above is the right instinct. It is not the finished argument.

Go deeper: the filter that is most dangerous

"Is this gene already linked to the phenotype?" is the most powerful filter on the list and the one most likely to mislead you, because it can only ever return genes somebody has already found. Apply it and you will never discover a new disease gene: the answer is guaranteed in advance to be something already known.

That is often the right trade. A clinical laboratory wants an answer today and is not trying to discover anything. But it should be a choice you made rather than a default you inherited, and the honest way to write it up is to say the analysis was restricted to known genes.

Two other places this reasoning goes

Cancer: drivers and passengers

The comparison changes completely. You are no longer asking how a variant compares with the population, you are comparing the tumour against the patient's own normal tissue, so most of what they inherited subtracts out and what remains arose in the tumour. It is a large improvement, not a clean cancellation: tumour cells in the "normal" sample, and mosaic changes present in only some tissue, both put variants on the wrong side of the subtraction, and there are tools whose whole job is estimating how badly the normal is contaminated.

A tumour typically carries thousands of such changes, and almost all are passengers: real mutations that came along for the ride and do nothing. A handful are drivers. Population frequency has little to say about a change that arose in this tumour this year, so the evidence becomes different: is this gene hit far more often across many tumours than chance predicts, and is the pattern of damage the kind that would knock out a tumour suppressor or switch on an oncogene?

The lesson transfers exactly. The filter you choose is a hypothesis about the mechanism, and it decides what you are able to find.

Common disease: risk instead of cause

Everything above assumed one variant causing one condition. Most disease is not like that. A genome-wide association study compares hundreds of thousands of people and finds variants slightly more common in those with a condition. The effects are usually tiny: a few percent of extra risk, across a population.

The associated marker is not necessarily causal. Most association signals are carried by a marker that is merely inherited alongside the real culprit, because nearby variants travel together, so a hit usually tags a region rather than naming a variant. Most of those regions are not in genes at all: they sit in regulatory sequence, and the honest position after finding one is that you do not yet know which variant matters, which gene it acts on, or how. That question is what Section 6 is about.

Add many small effects together and you get a polygenic risk score. It is a useful number about a population and a much weaker one about a person, and it inherits every bias in who was sequenced to build it. A score trained mostly on European-ancestry genomes predicts poorly elsewhere, and it is worth being precise about why, because "just collect more data" is only part of it. The training sets are unbalanced, which is a data problem. But allele frequencies differ between populations, so a marker that is common enough to measure in one may be rare in another; and which variants travel together differs too, so a marker that tags the causal variant in one population may not tag it in another. The last two are properties of the populations, not of the sample size.
