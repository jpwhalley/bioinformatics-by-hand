What exactly is simulated?

The browser generates 8,200 reads at about 30.7× nominal depth, builds a live pileup from them, and applies a caller to that pileup, so every number downstream derives from one dataset. But there is no FastQC, BWA, SAMtools or bcftools here: their terminal output is scripted to behave the way the real tools do, and the caller that produces your variant table is a simplified one with an invented quality formula, not bcftools.

② What the caller actually did

This page's simplified caller stacks the reads that cover a position, counts the letters in that column, and chooses the genotype that best explains those counts. Real callers keep that core idea and add more: they model base and mapping uncertainty, and may locally assemble candidate haplotypes or apply learned error models to the read evidence.

One summary worth computing on any call set is its substitution spectrum, for example the transition/transversion ratio. It can reveal unusual technical or biological patterns, but only against an appropriate expectation for the assay and genomic context: real germline spectra are not flat, with transitions enriched over transversions.

↓ Go deeper: why the indel is the hard one

A substitution is easy to count, because every read agrees on where it is. An insertion or deletion is not, because the aligner has to decide where the gap goes before anything can be counted, and inside a run of identical bases there is no way to tell one placement from another. Delete a G from GGGGG and you get GGGG whichever G you deleted.

That is why homopolymers and short tandem repeats are where callers disagree with each other, and it is the same problem you met in Section 1, where a gap could sit in several places for the same score. The difference is that here nobody is looking.

What the aligner does about it

BWA scores every placement and reports its confidence as MAPQ, which the SAM specification defines as -10 log10 of the probability that the placement is wrong. Internally BWA computes it from the margin between the best alignment score and the second best, and there is one line in its source that decides this read's fate:

if (sub >= a->score) return 0;

If the second-best placement scores at least as well as the best, the margin is gone and the answer is MAPQ 0. Not a low score: zero. The aligner is telling you it cannot distinguish the best placement from the alternatives.

Under a caller with a higher default it would have been thrown away before the caller saw it.

Uncertainty does not disappear; it changes form. A permissive pipeline attaches the read to an arbitrary reported location and shows you a result. A stricter one discards it and shows you silence. A filter you did not set, in a tool you did not write, decided which of the two you got.

And here is the part that should worry you

Your file says the same thing about both, which is to say nothing at all. You ran bcftools call -v, and -v asks for variant sites only, so neither position has a record. Absence from a variants-only VCF is genuinely ambiguous: it can mean the evidence supported the reference, or that there was no usable evidence.

That ambiguity is a property of what you asked for, not a limitation of the format. VCF can represent missing genotypes, with ./., and gVCF workflows go further and record reference confidence at non-variant positions precisely so that "we looked and saw reference" can be distinguished from "we could not look". If the question you are answering is whether a gene is clear, a variants-only VCF is the wrong output to be reading.

No record is not the same as no variant. Before calling a result negative, check coverage, mapping quality and whether the region was callable at all.

Go deeper: would longer reads fix this?

Partly. Longer reads generally improve unique placement because they carry more context, and that is a real reason long-read sequencing exists. They do not solve every repeat: a read wholly contained within a long, highly similar repeated region may remain ambiguous even when it is much longer. And errors cut the other way: a sequencing error, or a genuine difference between this genome and the reference, costs score at the true locus, narrows the margin over the second-best placement, and can collapse the aligner's confidence without the read moving at all. Which is why an unusual genome can be as hard to map as a noisy read.

How this runs at genome scale

On real data the ⏳ steps run for hours, so nobody types them at a prompt and waits: they go into a short script handed to the cluster's queue, which runs it on a compute node and emails you when it finishes. The wrapper syntax differs by site (SLURM, SGE, PBS, the cloud schedulers), so learn it from whoever runs your cluster; it takes about ten minutes once you know which commands to run and in what order, which is what this section was for.
