Two different orders, and they are not the same

Worth knowing before you start, because the module does not run in the order the laboratory does. What follows is one common short-read workflow, not the only one.

What actually happens
biological sample → library preparation → sequencing → base calling and demultiplexing → FASTQ → alignment → counts, variants or peaks, with quality control at each handover rather than at one point
The order here
we start with alignment, by hand, then go back to the raw data

Alignment comes first because it is the conceptual engine: once you have decided by hand where a read belongs and what that decision cost, everything else in the pipeline is a variation on it. But the sequencer produces FASTQ files, not alignments, and nothing has been aligned at the point the machine finishes. Do not read the section numbering as the order of a laboratory workflow.

How these work

Each section is a single file. Download it, double-click it, and it opens in your browser. There is no install, no login, and no internet needed once the file is on your machine.

Every section keeps a lab notebook as you go, recording what you did and what you found. It is yours. Nothing is collected and nothing is submitted. If you want to keep a copy, press Download notebook before you close the page. Each section tells you when it is finished and what to open next.

Nothing you do in these pages is transmitted anywhere. The notebook is written in your browser and saved only when you press the button.

About the data

The sequences, reads, quality scores, alignments, variant calls and family in these sections are simulated. They are built to behave the way real data behaves: the error profile, the depth, the quality drop along a read, the awkward indel in a homopolymer. The pages contain no donor-derived reads, genotypes or family data.

That is a deliberate choice rather than a shortcut. Sequencing data can be public: plenty sits openly in the Sequence Read Archive. But individual-level human data usually requires controlled access, through repositories such as dbGaP or the EGA, depending on what the donors consented to and what the data could reveal about them. A teaching module is not a reason to move data out of that arrangement.

Simulating preserves much of the reasoning while the data itself carries no one's genome. Not all of it: see below for what simulation takes away.

Where the numbers came from

The simulator is seeded, so everyone gets the same reads and therefore the same answer, and so a result can be reproduced when you come back to it. Read lengths, quality decay, depth, adapter rate, duplication and error profile were set to match published characteristics of real runs, and every threshold quoted in the module is taken from a real standard with the source named beside it.

What simulation takes away is worth naming. These data contain exactly the problems they were written to contain, and nothing else. Real runs bring unexpected contamination, sample swaps, metadata that turns out to be wrong, and tools that behave differently from their documentation. Some failure modes are built in here on purpose, including a batch effect and a set of damaged cells, because they are the point of the sections they sit in. The ones nobody thought of are exactly what is missing, and that gap is the difference between this and a real dataset.

Accessibility

Every section is operable by keyboard alone, works with a screen reader, reflows to a narrow screen without horizontal scrolling, and never uses colour as the only way to tell you something. If any part of this is not working for you, say so and it gets fixed. That is a bug in the module, not something to work around.

Questions

These sections are built to raise more questions than they answer. That is deliberate, and the questions are the valuable part. Keep them as you go: your notebook is the obvious place, and the ones you cannot resolve are exactly what to bring to whoever is teaching you.
