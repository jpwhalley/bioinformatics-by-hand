Interactive bioinformatics module

Follow sequencing data from raw reads to biological claims. At each step, decide what the result supports, and what it does not.

The recurring problem Compare observations with references, turn matches into measurements, and ask what each rule may have discarded. You begin by moving a 12 base read along a reference with your own hands, and end by applying the same reasoning to an unfamiliar assay.

Before you start

Nothing to install. Each section is a single file. Keep the files in one folder and open them in your browser: no login, and no internet needed once they are on your machine.

The main route stands on its own. Collapsed panels come in two kinds. Optional depth holds algorithms, equations and edge cases; skipping those leaves no gap. Table views hold the same data as a chart, in a form you can read, search and reach with a keyboard. These are not optional extras. They are an equal route to the same content, and for some readers the better one.

Your notebook stays with you. Each section builds a lab notebook of what you did and decided. Nothing you do in these pages is transmitted anywhere: the notebook is written in your browser and saved as a text file only when you press Download notebook. It is not saved automatically, so download it before closing a page. Record unresolved questions in it; they are what to bring to whoever is teaching you.

All individual-level reads, genotypes and family data are synthetic. What that preserves and what it costs is under About the simulated data, below.

Two orders. The module does not run in the order a laboratory does.

What actually happens
biological sample → library preparation → sequencing → base calling and demultiplexing → FASTQ → alignment → counts, variants or peaks, with quality control at each handover. This is one common short-read workflow, not the only one.
The order here
alignment first, by hand, because it is the idea everything else is built on, then back to the raw data. Read the section numbers as a teaching order, not a laboratory one.

Accessibility. Every section works by keyboard alone, with a screen reader, and on a narrow screen, and colour is never the only signal. Anything here that does not work for you is a bug to report, not something to work around.

About the simulated data

The sequences, reads, quality scores, alignments, variant calls, libraries, cells and family in these sections are simulated, built to behave the way real data behaves. The pages contain no donor-derived reads, genotypes or family data.

Sequencing data can be public: plenty sits openly in the Sequence Read Archive. But individual-level human data usually requires controlled access, through repositories such as dbGaP or the EGA, depending on what the donors consented to and what the data could reveal about them. Simulating preserves much of the reasoning while the data itself carries no one's genome.

The simulator is seeded, so everyone gets the same reads and the same answers. Where a published threshold is used, its source is named beside it; model-derived, simulator and teaching thresholds are presented as what they are. What simulation takes away is worth naming. These data contain exactly the problems they were written to contain. Real runs bring unexpected contamination, sample swaps, metadata that turns out to be wrong, and tools that behave differently from their documentation. The problems nobody thought of are exactly what is missing.
