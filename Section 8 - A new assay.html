Question one: what is being matched?

A droplet experiment aims to put one cell and one barcoded bead into each of many thousands of partitions. It does not manage it every time: most partitions end up with no cell in them at all, and a minority get two. Every transcript captured in a partition picks up that bead's barcode, so after sequencing you can tell which reads came from the same partition. Whether that partition held one intact cell is a different question, and it is question two. Here is what one read pair actually looks like.

The numbers, and which chemistry they belong to

10x Genomics Single Cell 3' chemistry puts the cell barcode and the UMI in Read 1 and the cDNA in Read 2. The bead oligo runs: a Read 1 primer, then the barcode, then the UMI, then an anchored poly-dT that captures polyadenylated transcripts. Because priming starts at the polyA tail, the sequence you get is the 3' end of the transcript, not the whole thing.

The lengths depend on the version, and quoting the wrong one is a common error. v2 used a 16 base barcode and a 10 base UMI, read in 26 cycles. v3, v3.1 and GEM-X v4 use a 16 base barcode and a 12 base UMI, read in 28 cycles. The original 2017 paper describes a 14 base barcode with a 10 base UMI, which belongs to a superseded chemistry, and is nonetheless what gets cited. Superseded is not the same as gone: those datasets are public and still get reanalysed, so the number matters the moment you open somebody else's FASTQ.

This section uses 16 and 12. The eight base sample index above is not part of that structure. It is Section 3's index, reused so the demultiplexing you have already done stays visible on the same read. A real v3.1 dual index library is sequenced as 28 cycles of Read 1, ten cycles of i7 and ten of i5, and 90 cycles of Read 2: two indexes, both ten bases, and neither of them eight.

Those matches are error tolerant, not exact

Saying a barcode is matched against a list makes it sound like string equality. It is not. Cell Ranger first counts how often every barcode on the inclusion list appears in the data. For an observed barcode that is not on the list but sits one base away from one that is, it computes the posterior probability that the difference is a sequencing error, using the base quality score at the mismatching position. If that probability exceeds 0.975 the observed barcode is replaced by the list barcode. The corrected sequence is written to the CB tag of the BAM and the original is kept in CR.

UMIs get the same treatment inside each barcode and gene group: two UMIs one base apart are taken to be one molecule that picked up a sequencing error, and the one with less read support is corrected to the more common one.

This is Section 2's scoring and Section 4's mapping quality applied to a sixteen base string. A near miss is evidence about the true answer rather than a failure to match. Every one of those corrections is a decision made on your behalf, and each has a threshold in it.

Count the matching operations on that one read. The sample index against a sample sheet. The cell barcode against a list of valid barcodes. The UMI against the other UMIs on the same gene in the same partition. And the cDNA against the genome. Four matches, three of which are a short string against a list, exactly what you did by hand in Section 1 and by index in Section 3, and like those, none of the three is a plain equality test: a near miss gets scored, and may get corrected. The fourth is the alignment from Section 4, with the splicing from Section 7. Single-cell RNA-seq did not introduce a new operation. It introduced two more barcodes.

Why a high mitochondrial fraction means something different here

In Section 6 the mitochondrial fraction was a property of a library, and it told you about the protocol. In a single cell it is a property of a cell, and a high value can be consistent with a damaged or dying one: the plasma membrane broke, cytoplasmic messenger RNA leaked out, and what is left is disproportionately the transcripts held inside mitochondria. Luecken and Theis describe these as "cells whose cytoplasmic mRNA has leaked out through a broken membrane, and thus, only mRNA located in the mitochondria is still conserved".

Can be, not is. A high mitochondrial fraction is also ordinary biology in cells doing a lot of respiration, and what counts as ordinary varies by species, tissue, cell type, state and protocol. Osorio and Cai measured it systematically across human and mouse tissues and found proportions running from 0.17% to 14.64%; nearly a third of the 44 human tissues they examined exceeded 5%, which is why they propose 10% for human data and 5% for mouse rather than one number for everything. Look again at the strict rule on the plot above: its 5% ceiling is the threshold they argue against for human tissue. Their conclusion is that "omitting the mtDNA% QC filter or adopting a suboptimal mtDNA% threshold may lead to erroneous biological interpretations of scRNA-seq data".

So treat the number as evidence to investigate alongside the others, not as an instruction to delete a cell. What makes the middle group on the map safe to call damaged is not its mitochondrial fraction on its own; it is that several independent indicators agree, and you can check two of them in the table above. It has the fewest genes detected of any group and by a wide margin the highest mitochondrial fraction. One of those is a hint. Both of them pointing the same way, in cells that also carry the fewest molecules, is a finding.

Same number, different meaning, different action. In Section 6 you responded by changing protocol or accepting the loss of depth. Here the response is to look harder at those cells, and often to remove them. Notice that you cannot tell which meaning applies without knowing what the number is a property of.

Two failures with no equivalent in a bulk experiment

Ambient RNA. Cells that lysed before they were encapsulated leave their messages floating in the input suspension, and that soup is loaded into every droplet. So every barcode receives a background of transcripts from cells it is not. The effect is not subtle and not rare: the authors of SoupX note that it "is recognized that these contaminating non-endogenous RNAs are present even within datasets of the highest quality", and that it "may significantly confound the biological interpretation". You will see T cell markers in your monocytes. They are not there.

Doublets. Two cells in one droplet share a barcode and are reported as one cell with a hybrid transcriptome. The DoubletFinder authors put the danger precisely: doublets "may be interpreted as previously-undescribed cell types, developmental intermediates, or disease states". A doublet of a monocyte and a T cell is a beautiful, publishable, non-existent intermediate. The column you built earlier carried two LYZ molecules and two CD3D molecules in one barcode, which are markers of exactly those two lineages. At four molecules that is noise. The same pattern at four hundred molecules is this question, and you would have no way to tell it from a real hybrid cell by looking at the column.

Both can be estimated or mitigated computationally, by tools such as SoupX or CellBender for ambient RNA and Scrublet or DoubletFinder for doublets. Both are already in the count matrix, which is the difficulty: ambient transcripts are counted as though they belonged to the barcode that caught them, and a doublet's two cells are added together into a single column. What the matrix does not contain is a label saying which counts are ambient or which barcodes hold more than one cell. Those tools estimate that label. None of them recovers ground truth, and every one of them has a threshold in it.

And then, what the picture is for

With the experimental unit settled, an assay that measures thousands of cells separately supports questions bulk RNA-seq cannot ask, and those questions come with machinery of their own.

The standard first move is to squeeze each cell's few thousand measurements down to two numbers so they can be drawn, using UMAP or t-SNE. Cells that look alike land near each other, and groups become visible. Colour the same plot four different ways and see how much the colouring decides.

What you are allowed to read off this picture, and what you are not

You may use a picture like this to inspect how a chosen representation has organised the cells, and to overlay metadata or a quality metric on it, which is exactly what the four buttons above do. That is its job.

You may not treat islands, distances, areas, densities or apparent trajectories as measurements. A two dimensional embedding can manufacture apparent islands and tear real continua apart, so even "there are discrete groups here" is a claim to be checked in the underlying data and for stability across analysis choices, not read off the picture.

You may not read distances between groups, and you may not read group sizes. Both are artefacts of the layout, not measurements. Wattenberg and colleagues put both in section headings: "Cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot mean nothing" and "Distances between clusters might not mean anything". A cluster drawn twice as far away is not twice as different, and a cluster drawn twice as large does not contain twice as many cells, or twice as much variation.

Chari and Pachter go further, and are worth taking seriously before you put one of these in a paper: reducing "hundreds or thousands of dimensions to 2 inevitably induces significant distortion", and their measurements found the two-dimensional embedding disagreed with the real neighbours of a cell most of the time. Their recommendation is that such plots "should be treated more as cartoon diagrams to be displayed post-analysis".

Which is a reasonable description of what it is for. It is a way of showing somebody the structure you found by other means. It is not the evidence.
